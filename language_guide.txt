These guidelines provide a reference point for using inclusive and accessible language 
in storytelling and educational resources. They integrate insights from the Spirit of 2012 
project, UK industry standards, and best practices from respected organisations.

1. Use Person-First or Identity-First Language Thoughtfully
*	Respect Individual Preferences: Always ask individuals how they prefer to be 
described, ensuring that language reflects their identities and experiences 
(University of Edinburgh).
*	Person-first language places the individual before the disability (e.g., "a person 
with a disability").
*	Identity-first language (e.g., "disabled person") is often preferred within the 
disability rights community.
*	Avoid Assumptions: Do not assume a person's abilities, needs, or identity 
(University of Leeds).
2. Avoid Deficit-Based Language
*	Do not describe disability as something inherently negative or something to 
"overcome."
*	Avoid phrases like 'suffers from' or 'is afflicted with'. Instead, use neutral 
descriptions such as "lives with" or "has a disability" (Spirit of 2012 Evidence 
Synthesis).
*	Do not frame disability in a way that centres non-disabled people�s perspectives 
(e.g., "despite their disability").
3. Emphasize Capabilities and Strengths
*	Representation should focus on the diverse skills, abilities, and contributions of 
disabled individuals.
*	Highlight participation, independence, and leadership rather than solely 
challenges or barriers (Spirit of 2012 Evidence Synthesis).
4. Encourage Non-Binary Thinking About Ability
*	Avoid framing ability as a strict binary (disabled/non-disabled).
*	Use language that acknowledges a spectrum of abilities and diverse experiences 
(Spirit of 2012 Evidence Synthesis).
5. Use Inclusive Terminology for Communication and Cognition
*	Use gender-neutral terms (e.g., "partner" instead of "husband" or "wife") 
(Oxfam).
*	Avoid ageist language (e.g., use "older adults" instead of "the elderly") (Learning 
for Involvement).
*	Ensure language accommodates various communication modes, including 
assistive technology.
*	Use plain language to improve accessibility for individuals with cognitive 
disabilities (University of Leeds).
6. Respect Cultural and Linguistic Diversity
*	Recognise that disability terminology varies across cultures.
*	Ensure translations and multilingual resources maintain respect and inclusivity.
7. Avoid Stereotyping and "Inspiration Porn"
*	Do not use disabled people's experiences solely as a source of inspiration for 
non-disabled audiences.
*	Represent disabled individuals as fully developed characters with agency and 
complexity (MediaCat).
8. Encourage Reflexivity and Self-Awareness
*	Use language that encourages reflection and critical thinking about societal 
attitudes toward disability.
*	Ensure non-disabled audiences understand their own limitations in knowledge 
and experience (Spirit of 2012 Evidence Synthesis).
9. Foster Representation in Leadership and Decision-Making
*	Ensure that disabled voices are involved in creating and shaping narratives.
*	Co-produce content with disabled individuals (Spirit of 2012 Evidence Synthesis).
10. Make Digital and Print Content Accessible
*	Use plain language and avoid jargon.
*	Provide alternative formats, such as audio descriptions, subtitles, and easy-read 
versions.
*	Ensure text and background color contrast meets accessibility guidelines.
*	Design for accessibility from the outset (MediaCat).
11. Challenge Biases and Promote Inclusive Environments
*	Challenge unconscious biases: Use language that promotes inclusion and 
avoids reinforcing stereotypes.
*	Ensure diverse representation: Stories and content should reflect the diversity 
of society (University of Leeds).

By following these principles, we ensure that our storytelling and educational resources 
are respectful, inclusive, and aligned with UK best practices. This document serves as a 
foundational reference for fostering authentic representation and equitable 
communication.
