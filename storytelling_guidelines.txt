AI Storytelling Guidelines (Ages 2–5)
1. Purpose

AI-generated stories must:

Be suitable for toddlers and preschoolers (ages 2–5).

Support language, creativity, social-emotional growth, and inclusive literacy.

Be engaging, simple, and developmentally appropriate for short attention spans.

Provide comfort and reassurance, even if mild conflict is introduced.

Stay within a length of 250–600 words.

2. Story Elements

Always include:

Characters

Simple, relatable, often anthropomorphic animals or children.

Small cast (1 main character + a few supporting).

Express clear traits/emotions (happy, worried, curious).

Representation should be diverse (different cultures, abilities, family structures).

Rule: Characters must be multi-dimensional; difference (e.g. ability, culture) is part of who they are, but not their only trait.

Plot

Simple, linear, and chronological.

One main event, problem, or goal, followed by a gentle resolution.

Conflict should be brief, gentle, and resolved with co-operation, comfort, or play.

Use clear cause-and-effect relationships.

Endings must be safe, reassuring, and satisfying.

Setting

Familiar places (home, park, school, forest) or simple fantasy settings.

Use 1–2 main locations.

Describe with a few vivid sensory details (light, sound, touch).

Language

UK English.

Short sentences, grouped into 2–4 sentence paragraphs.

Use rhythm, rhyme, and sound words (e.g. squelch-squerch, flap-flap).

Keep vocabulary simple and concrete.

Tone

Warm, playful, reassuring.

Light humour or mild suspense allowed, but always resolved quickly.

Sensory Language

Add sight, sound, touch, taste, or smell.

Keep concise and familiar (“the cake smelled sweet like strawberries”).

3. Narrative Structures

Choose one structure per story:

Circular: Story ends where it began (e.g. home to home).

Cumulative/Repetitive: Repeating patterns that build (e.g. “and then…”).

Episodic: Short, linked scenes (mini-adventures).

Simple Linear: Beginning → middle → end with clear resolution.

Interactive/Hybrid: Invite participation (questions, refrains, sound actions).

4. Storytelling Techniques

Use repetition sparingly: repeat a key phrase 1–3 times for rhythm and participation.

Provide distinct voices for characters (small mouse = squeaky, big bear = deep).

Use pauses to build anticipation.

Include at least one participatory element:

A repeated refrain

A sound effect the child can echo

A simple question or prediction

5. Inclusivity Rules

Ensure diverse representation (race, culture, ability, family structures).

Avoid stereotypes and deficit framing.

Normalize difference: show varied ways of moving, communicating, or playing.

Pronoun use: For stories intended for direct child audiences (ages 2–5), assign each character a consistent pronoun (he or she) unless instructed otherwise.

Bad example: “Timmy couldn’t play because he was disabled, but his friends helped him anyway.”

Good example: “Timmy played his own way—he zoomed down the hill on his wheels, while the others ran beside him.”

Focus on shared play, co-operation, and unique strengths.

6. Developmental Anchors

Stories should support:

Language and Communication: new vocabulary, rhythm, dialogue, repetition.

Social-Emotional Skills: empathy, sharing, belonging, recognising feelings.

Imagination and Creativity: playful worlds, pretend scenarios, character-led journeys.

Inclusive Literacy: mirrors (self-representation) and windows (seeing others).

Cognitive Development: sequencing (first, then, next), memory, cause/effect.

7. Endings

Always end with reassurance, comfort, or reflection.

Use child-like reflective questions, such as:

“Have you ever felt left out?”

“What makes you smile?”

“Who do you like to play with?”

Keep questions open and simple, encouraging empathy and participation.
